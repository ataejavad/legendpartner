The Journal — No. 04 — Essay

The scarcity nobody names: time, not options.

7 minutes · Written by the house

This essay accompanies our position on curation. It is written for members and for those considering an engagement.

Ask a successful person in their forties why they are not in the relationship they want, and you will almost never hear that they cannot find candidates. You will hear something quieter, and much harder to solve.

They will describe a calendar that is committed eleven weeks out. A social circle that has, without anyone deciding it, become entirely professional. Three cities in a month. A reluctance — reasonable, in their position — to be seen looking. And a growing suspicion that the process available to everyone else was not designed with their life in mind.

The market solved the wrong problem

For fifteen years, the relationship industry has optimised for supply. More profiles, faster matching, wider reach, better filters. It has been enormously successful at what it set out to do. Meeting someone has never been easier.

But supply was not the constraint. For an accomplished, well-connected person, the constraint has always been the cost of evaluation — the hours, the attention, the exposure, and the emotional expenditure required to discover, three months in, that two lives were never going to fit.

Abundance does not reduce the cost of a bad decision. It multiplies the number of decisions you must make.

This is why the same people who manage billion-dollar allocations with rigour approach the most consequential decision of their lives with no method at all. Not from carelessness — from the absence of any respectable process to use.

What a search actually costs

Consider the arithmetic honestly. A conventional route requires you to review many candidates, meet a fraction of them, and discover compatibility empirically, in person, over months. Each round costs an evening, a degree of exposure, and the small accumulating fatigue of hope tested against reality.

Now consider the alternative: someone whose profession is to understand you properly, who has met each candidate, who knows what a life like yours requires, and who will bring you one person with reasons attached. The number of evenings involved falls by an order of magnitude. The quality of each one rises.

That is the whole economic argument for curation, and it has nothing to do with exclusivity for its own sake.

The second scarcity: candour

There is a second thing our members are short of, and it is rarer than time. Almost no one in their life is positioned to tell them the truth about this subject.

Colleagues will not. Family have an interest. Friends of long standing have their own account of who you are, formed years ago. The result is that a person can spend a decade looking for something that they have never once been asked to describe precisely — or challenged on when the description does not hold together.

A good advisor does both. Not with theories about love, but with the same directness you would expect from any professional you retain for judgment rather than agreement.

What this implies for how the work should be done

If time and candour are the scarce resources, then the design of the service follows almost mechanically. Fewer introductions, considered far longer. Every candidate met in person, because character does not survive translation to a document. Reasons given for each recommendation, so that a member can disagree intelligently. And absolute privacy — because the moment a search becomes visible, its cost to a public person exceeds any possible benefit.

None of this is romantic. It is the unglamorous mechanics of doing something properly. What it produces, at its best, is not efficiency at all — it is the possibility of an introduction that would never have occurred, to someone you would never have found, with a reason behind it that holds up on the third meeting as well as the first.

That is the only outcome we consider worth measuring.
